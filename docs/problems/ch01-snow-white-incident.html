Chapter 1

Snow White Investigates the Doc Incident (Diagnostic)

Six months after Snow White stood up the security awareness program at Seven Dwarfs Mining, click-through on simulated phishing is down 80%. The board is pleased. The Q3 audit committee thanked her by name.

Then, on a Friday afternoon, the company loses $2.3 million to a wire-transfer fraud.

Snow White is now drafting the 72-hour board report. Her task is no longer to design the program — it is to walk backward through what happened and name the specific governance control that failed at each step. The lesson she wants the board to take is uncomfortable: an awareness program is necessary, not sufficient. Around it has to sit a documentation hierarchy, an unambiguous RACI, and a culture of enforcement that does not bend for executives.

Post-incident report — wire-transfer fraud, 2026-09-11.   Author: S. White, GRC Analyst. Status: draft for board. Do not forward.

What happened

Friday 14:32 — vishing call to Doc (CEO) from a spoofed CFO number. Caller claimed an urgent acquisition wire to Queen's Royal Bank and pressed for action 'before market close.' Doc verbally authorized a $2.3M wire to the Treasury team without calling the CFO back. Treasury executed by 16:18. Recovery to date: $0.

Contributing failures

  • Doc was granted a permanent exception from quarterly phishing simulations and the awareness program. The justification on file reads: 'CEO doesn't have time.'
  • The program did not establish any mandatory call-back-to-known-number rule for wires above $50,000. Treasury followed Doc's verbal instruction because they had nothing written that said they shouldn't.
  • 'Who is authorized to override the wire-approval threshold?' was never resolved during program rollout. Treasury escalated to Doc; Doc was the only approver named — both requester and approver in the same person.
  • Doc also held a permanent MFA exception on his executive email. The CFO's account (used by the attacker to harvest the context that made the call credible) had been accessed from an unfamiliar IP two days earlier. No alert fired; nobody was watching.
  • Helpdesk received a near-identical vishing call to Grumpy in Accounting the previous week. He reported it via the awareness program's Report Phishing button as he'd been trained. The report sat in a queue. No advisory was issued. Nobody was looking.

What this means

The simulated-phishing click-rate metric on my Q3 dashboard was real, but it described the workforce. It did not describe the executive who was actually targeted, the procedure that was actually executed, or the report that was actually filed.

Working backward from each contributing failure in the post-incident report, identify the governance control that failed.

Doc's exemption from training and simulations is — independent of any document tier — a failure of . The chapter specifically warned that exceptions for senior leaders dissolve the program in the eyes of every other employee.

The absent mandatory call-back rule for wires above $50,000 should have been written into a wire-approval , since the requirement is mandatory, measurable, and tied to a numeric threshold.

Doc was both the Responsible requester and the only Accountable approver for the wire. That overlap is exactly what the chapter's ownership tool, the wire-transfer , exists to expose before an incident.

The attack technique itself — a phone call impersonating an executive to authorize a large transfer — is best classified as .

The earlier Grumpy report sitting unactioned in a queue is a failure of the awareness program's — a one-way path from reporter to a black hole rather than a closed-loop process that produces an advisory.

Snow White's headline recommendation — dual approval with mandatory call-back for any wire above the threshold, with no exceptions for executives — applies the principle of , closing both the procedural and the cultural gap simultaneously.
