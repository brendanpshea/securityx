Chapter 0: How to Read This Book

Threats Real and Imagined: A Cybersecurity Casebook for SecurityX is an openly licensed textbook and practice-problem collection for the CompTIA SecurityX (CAS-005) certification — the senior-level cybersecurity exam that succeeded CASP+ in 2024. It is freely available online for self-study and adoptable as a course textbook. The book is by Brendan Shea, PhD.

This short chapter explains what the book is, who it’s for, how it’s organized, and how you can use — and reuse — it.

Who This Book Is For

Three audiences in roughly equal measure:

  • Students preparing for the CAS-005 exam. Most readers will arrive having already passed Security+ and CySA+ and are looking for a coherent next step rather than another grab bag of exam questions.
  • Instructors teaching senior-level cybersecurity or capstone courses who want a license-friendly text they can adapt, excerpt, or remix.
  • Practitioners who want a structured refresher on the modern CASP+/SecurityX domain — governance, secure architecture, identity, cryptography, network and endpoint engineering, specialized and OT environments, and security operations.

The book assumes the reader is comfortable with the material of an introductory security course. It does not re-teach the OSI model, basic cryptography, or how a TCP handshake works; it treats those as prior knowledge and builds on them.

How the Book Is Organized

Twelve chapters, each following a consistent shape so readers always know where they are:

  • Learning outcomes mapped to Bloom’s taxonomy and aligned to specific CAS-005 objectives, listed at the top of each chapter.
  • A Practice-Based Question (PBQ) — a scenario-driven decision exercise featuring a recurring cast — linked at the start. The PBQ is the heaviest applied exercise for the chapter.
  • Core narrative content organized around guiding questions (“How do we…?”, “What is…?”, “Why does…?”) rather than topic dumps.
  • Quick Checks at the end of each major section: three short prompts using a Recall / Discriminate / Apply rubric, with answers folded inline for self-pacing.
  • Fictional case studies featuring characters drawn from literature and folklore — Snow White as a GRC analyst, Fitzwilliam Darcy as a risk director, Count Dracula as a CISO, Sherlock Holmes as a threat hunter. The cast is deliberate: memorable, evocative, and easy to keep straight across chapters, without trivializing the material.
  • Historical case studies of real-world breaches — Target, SolarWinds, Capital One, Equifax, DigiNotar, Log4Shell, XZ Utils, and others — because most of cybersecurity’s hard lessons are written in past incidents.
  • Diagrams and tables generated reproducibly from Python scripts so they can be regenerated, corrected, or remixed.
  • Synthesis Activities at the end of each chapter — five open-ended exercises (a practitioner interview, a structured debate, a current-events watch, a hands-on lab artifact, and a deeper applied exercise) designed to extend the chapter beyond what a search engine or chatbot can answer. Each activity works individually or in a group.

A few notes on what the book is not: it is not an exam-cram product — you can find those elsewhere, and you may want one to drill timing; it is not narrowly platform-specific — the book teaches concepts that survive vendor change; and it is not a substitute for hands-on practice — lab activities are scaffolded, but the work is yours.

Alignment to CompTIA SecurityX (CAS-005)

Every chapter names the CAS-005 objectives it covers. A consolidated mapping of all objectives to chapter sections lives at CAS-005 Alignment. The book covers the full exam blueprint at the senior-practitioner depth the credential expects.

What the book does not try to be is a question bank. The Synthesis Activities and PBQs build the integrative thinking the exam tests; for raw timed-question practice, pair this book with a third-party question bank.

A Note on Using Generative AI With This Book

The cybersecurity field — both as a domain and as a daily practice — is being reshaped by generative AI. This book does not pretend otherwise.

Several Synthesis Activities include sidebars showing concrete, productive uses of a chatbot: rehearsing an interview before speaking with a real practitioner, sparring against your own written argument from the opposite position, facilitating a tabletop exercise by injecting realistic complications. In every case, the model is offered as a practice partner — never as a source.

The activities themselves remain the work. Where AI guidance appears, it is meant to model good practice; if you find yourself reaching for a chatbot to produce the deliverable rather than to sharpen it, the exercise has failed its purpose. The book also takes seriously that AI systems hallucinate, age out of date, and reflect their training data: every chapter encourages readers to ground claims in primary sources — vendor advisories, CISA bulletins, NIST publications, court filings — rather than in any model’s recollection.

AI Use Disclosure

This textbook was developed in close collaboration with Anthropic’s Claude (primarily Opus 4.7). The author drafted the chapter outlines, learning outcomes, pedagogical approach, character cast, and case-study selections; Claude was used to produce initial chapter prose to those specifications. All AI-generated content was substantially revised, fact-checked, restructured, and edited by the human author before publication. Diagrams were produced with Python (matplotlib / graphviz) using AI-assisted scripts that were then human-tuned for clarity.

The author takes full responsibility for the accuracy, framing, and pedagogical choices of the final text. Readers who find errors or want to suggest improvements are encouraged to open an issue or pull request on the project’s GitHub.
